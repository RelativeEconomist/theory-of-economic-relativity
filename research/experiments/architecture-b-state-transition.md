# Architecture B: State–Decision–Transition Economics

**Status:** Independent competing research proposal, draft 0.1.0  
**Comparison baseline:** Theory of Economic Relativity (TER), canonical version 0.5.0  
**Purpose:** Test whether a smaller, mathematically explicit economic architecture matches or exceeds TER's explanatory and modeling usefulness.  
**Not a TER amendment.** All symbols and axioms below belong only to Architecture B.

## Abstract

Architecture B describes an economic system as an evolving state, agents using information to choose actions, a realization mechanism producing consequences from joint actions, and a transition law mapping the system into subsequent states. It treats optimization, beliefs, valuation, expectations, equilibrium, institutions, and aggregation as structures to specify when an economic problem requires them, rather than necessarily distinct universal primitives. This is a competing *modeling architecture*, not a demonstrated new economic law. Its key vulnerability is that compact state descriptions can hide causal distinctions needed for identification, replication, or meaningful explanation.

## 1. Primitive objects and timing

For a discrete decision stage \(t\), define:

| Symbol | Meaning | Formal domain |
|---|---|---|
| \(N\) | Set of modeled agents | Finite set for the baseline specification |
| \(X_t\) | Objective system state before decisions at stage \(t\) | \(X_t\in\mathcal X\) |
| \(I_{i,t}\) | Information available to agent \(i\) at selection | \(I_{i,t}\in\mathcal I_i\) |
| \(A_i(I_{i,t})\) | Actions the agent's specified policy permits it to select given its information | Nonempty subset of \(\mathcal A_i\) |
| \(\pi_i\) | Agent decision rule, potentially randomized | \(\pi_i(\cdot\mid I_{i,t})\in\Delta(A_i(I_{i,t}))\) |
| \(a_{i,t}\) | Agent's selected action | \(a_{i,t}\in A_i(I_{i,t})\) |
| \(Y_t\) | Jointly realized economic outcome | \(Y_t\in\mathcal Y\) |
| \(\mathcal R\) | Realization kernel | \(\mathcal R(dy\mid X_t,a_t)\) |
| \(\mathcal Q\) | System transition kernel | \(\mathcal Q(dx'\mid X_t,a_t,Y_t)\) |

Here \(a_t=(a_{i,t})_{i\in N}\), \(\Delta(A)\) denotes probability distributions over actions, and a deterministic rule or realization is a special case of a probability kernel. Spaces are assumed measurable and the kernels well-defined. When existence or computation requires finite, compact, continuous, or otherwise restricted spaces, those conditions must be stated in the individual specification.

**Information discipline.** Each agent's information must be defined using its observations and history available **before** selection. It may depend on \(X_t\), previous actions and outcomes, and private signals, but not on unobserved future realizations. Agent information need not identify \(X_t\). A belief about \(X_t\) is a possible component of \(I_{i,t}\), not a guarantee of accuracy.

**State discipline.** \(X_t\) contains economically relevant objective variables necessary for the transition specification (for example, inventories, capital, legal constraints, contractual commitments, prices when determined before selection). A complete Markov state may be large, history-dependent in its construction, or infeasible to observe. A low-dimensional state is a substantive approximation, not automatically valid.

### Three governing relationships

**Decision:**

\[
a_{i,t}\sim\pi_i(\cdot\mid I_{i,t}),\qquad a_{i,t}\in A_i(I_{i,t}).
\]

**Realization:**

\[
Y_t\sim\mathcal R(\cdot\mid X_t,a_t).
\]

**Transition:**

\[
X_{t+1}\sim\mathcal Q(\cdot\mid X_t,a_t,Y_t).
\]

The information process and initial-state distribution are **also required for a complete executable specification**. They are model-specific data-generating mechanisms, even though they are not elevated to a fourth headline formula. Without them the architecture is not yet a stochastic process.

**Timing:** initial state and decision information → actions → realized outcomes → next objective state → new observations. Ordered or asynchronous moves are represented as successive stages, with newly available information at each stage. A stage cannot silently include future information in current choices.

## 2. Proposed axioms

These are *Architecture B* assumptions, offered for critique rather than asserted as universal truths.

**B1 — Informational nonanticipation.** A selected action is generated from the information available to the selecting agent at that decision stage. The selection rule cannot condition on an unobserved future outcome.

**B2 — Well-defined selection.** For every modeled decision event, the specification declares a nonempty set of actions the agent can select and a rule that selects an action (or a probability distribution) from that set. Optimization is optional.

**B3 — Joint realization.** A model's realized outcomes are generated by an explicitly specified mechanism depending on the objective system state and the relevant joint action profile; unmodeled factors must be stated as uncertainty or external inputs, not retrospectively invented to fit a result.

**B4 — Dynamic consistency.** Changes in objective system state follow an explicitly specified transition mechanism conditioned on the prior state, selected actions, and realized outcomes. A state description is adequate only relative to the forecasting or explanation task under consideration.

B2 and parts of B3–B4 are primarily **model admissibility requirements**, not empirically distinctive economic laws. Their value is formal auditability. A serious comparison must determine whether the additional distinctions enforced by TER have more explanatory value than this compactness.

## 3. Economic mechanisms as optional specifications

- **Optimization and preferences:** Define a model-specific payoff \(u_i\) and a criterion, then require \(\pi_i\) to solve the stated decision problem; neither optimization nor a common payoff is imposed universally.
- **Beliefs and learning:** Define signals, a belief state, and updating rules within \(I_i\). If the research question concerns belief errors, represent and measure them explicitly instead of hiding them inside \(\pi_i\).
- **Subjective action availability:** \(A_i(I_i)\) is what the policy treats as selectable; it is **not** the objective realization constraint. The consequences of an attempted but objectively infeasible action are handled by \(\mathcal R\). This functional distinction exists but, unlike TER, does not have an independently protected universal primitive for perceived feasibility.
- **Markets and institutions:** Include price-setting, contractual enforcement, production, matching, clearing, rationing, or institutional rules explicitly in \(\mathcal R\), the state, or sequential decision stages, according to their causal timing. Do not use one unspecified `market mechanism` to absorb mismatches.
- **Uncertainty:** Randomness can enter the information process, \(\pi_i\), \(\mathcal R\), or \(\mathcal Q\), with independent declarations and reproducible seeds in computational work. Never interpret stochastic notation as evidence that the underlying world is intrinsically random.

## 4. Equilibrium is an optional restriction, not an axiom

For a dynamic strategic specification with declared agent payoff functionals \(J_i\), an equilibrium policy profile \(\pi^*\) may be defined by:

\[
J_i(\pi_i^*,\pi_{-i}^*)\ge J_i(\pi_i,\pi_{-i}^*)
\quad\text{for all admissible deviations }\pi_i\text{ and each }i.
\]

The admissible deviations, information sets, expectation rules, and solution concept (for example, Nash, subgame-perfect, Markov-perfect, or recursive competitive equilibrium) must be declared. This inequality is **not** a universal equilibrium definition for all applications. Market clearing, budget feasibility, and accounting identities are added only where relevant and independently justified. No general existence or uniqueness theorem is claimed without further assumptions.

## 5. Micro-to-macro relationship

Define a model-specific aggregation map \(\mathcal L\):

\[
Z_t=\mathcal L(X_t,Y_t,a_t).
\]

For example, aggregate consumption may equal the sum of recorded realized individual consumption, provided the underlying quantities are consistently defined. This is an accounting identity, not proof that a closed autonomous macroeconomic law exists.

A **closed macro transition** \(Z_{t+1}\sim\overline{\mathcal Q}(\cdot\mid Z_t)\) exists only when two different underlying microstates with the same \(Z_t\) imply the same conditional law of \(Z_{t+1}\), under the specified policies and transition mechanisms (a lumpability/sufficiency condition). If they do not, macro outcomes require additional microstate information or an approximate reduced-form model. This is a critical, testable limitation on any claim that micro and macro are automatically unified.

**Minimal counterexample:** Two economies each hold total productive capital of 10. In Economy 1, the capital belongs to an agent able to invest it; in Economy 2, it belongs to an agent facing a binding credit or authority restriction. Identical total capital does not imply identical next-period aggregate output. The distribution and institutional constraints matter. Thus \(Z_t=\text{total capital}\) is not generally a sufficient macro state.

## 6. Worked mapping: one-period consumer choice

**Original model:** A consumer selects quantities \((x_1,x_2)\) to maximize \(u(x_1,x_2)\) subject to \(p_1x_1+p_2x_2\le m\), \(x_1,x_2\ge0\).

**Architecture B specification:**

- \(X_t=(p_1,p_2,m)\), treated as known objective conditions for this single-agent example.
- \(I_{i,t}=X_t\) (perfect observation **assumed for this example**).
- \(A_i(I_{i,t})=\{(x_1,x_2)\in\mathbb R_+^2:p_1x_1+p_2x_2\le m\}\).
- \(\pi_i\) selects an \(\arg\max_{x\in A_i(I_{i,t})}u(x)\), with a specified tie-break rule; existence requires suitable assumptions on \(u\), prices, and income.
- \(\mathcal R\) records the consumed bundle and spending as \(Y_t\), assuming fulfillment as requested. \(\mathcal Q\) is unnecessary for this strictly one-period example.

**Comparison limitation:** This exact textbook setup cannot test Architecture B's ability to separate perceived feasibility from objective feasibility, because perfect observation and successful fulfillment were assumed. A stronger experiment must introduce mistaken budget information or unavailable inventory and compare predictions without changing each architecture after seeing the outcomes.

## 7. Adversarial comparison protocol against TER v0.5.0

Use **identical underlying economic tasks and evidence**, with separate prior specifications under both architectures:

1. **Static consumer choice:** Preserve the original utility, budget, and chosen bundle; then test information errors and failed execution.
2. **Strategic market interaction:** Two sellers choose prices, with explicit demand allocation, timing, and an agreed equilibrium concept. Verify price and quantity outcomes and counterfactuals.
3. **Dynamic micro-to-macro economy:** Heterogeneous agents save, invest, or consume under explicit constraints; derive aggregate capital/output and test whether the proposed macro state is sufficient.

For each test, pre-register the model components, which inputs are observed versus inferred, and the same held-out outcomes. Evaluate: (i) reproduction of established results, (ii) independent estimability and identification, (iii) predictive/counterfactual performance, (iv) number and clarity of assumptions, (v) computational requirements, (vi) robustness to misspecification, and (vii) whether a meaningful economic mechanism can be represented without redefining primitives.

**Potential falsifiers of Architecture B's advantage:** If compact information states require repeatedly restoring TER-style belief/feasibility/valuation distinctions to identify mechanisms, or if simplified macro states fail to preserve transition behavior across the target examples, the case for Architecture B's claimed parsimony weakens. Conversely, if Architecture B matches TER's validated results under fewer independent commitments and clearer identification, it creates a serious challenge to TER's architectural necessity.

## 8. Open problems and explicit limits

1. **State sufficiency:** Does an economical, observable \(X_t\) exist for the chosen class of problems, or is the true state effectively the complete history?
2. **Information identification:** Can separately measured beliefs and choice sets be recovered when represented only inside \(I_i\) and \(A_i\)?
3. **Restrictions versus flexibility:** Without independently constrained \(\pi\), \(\mathcal R\), and \(\mathcal Q\), Architecture B is no more predictive than an unconstrained TER specification.
4. **Emergence and aggregation:** Microeconomic representation does not guarantee a closed macro model, uniqueness of equilibrium, stability, or aggregate welfare properties.
5. **Normative neutrality:** Any welfare conclusion requires a separately declared welfare criterion and cannot follow from the architecture alone.
6. **Mathematical validation:** No general existence, identification, identification-consistency, or convergence results have been proven for Architecture B here.

## 10. Research decision

**Do not replace or modify TER v0.5.0 yet.** Architecture B currently wins only in syntactic compression and direct use of state-based mathematical machinery. It has **not** established better explanatory power, empirical performance, or mathematical necessity. The next milestone is to execute the three controlled mappings, including one with imperfect information and one where micro-to-macro aggregation fails unless a richer state is retained.
